## 2. Executive Summary

The AI governance landscape reached a pivotal inflection point this quarter. On 7 May 2026, EU co-legislators struck a landmark Omnibus deal that pushes standalone high-risk AI obligations to December 2027 — but crucially, transparency rules and GPAI requirements remain firmly live. In the UK, financial regulators are hardening accountability expectations under SMCR and Consumer Duty without introducing bespoke AI rules, while the FCA's Mills Review heads toward a summer 2026 board report. In Africa, South Africa published its Draft National AI Policy in April 2026 and an OECD/AU workshop convened 12 governments in Nairobi, signalling a continental shift from strategy to enforceable governance. Across all regions, enterprises face a common pressure point: board-level accountability structures are lagging behind rapidly scaling AI deployments.

## 3. Latest Developments

### D1 — EU AI Omnibus: High-Risk Deadline Pushed to December 2027

On 7 May 2026, the Council of the EU announced a provisional political agreement with the European Parliament on the "AI Omnibus," part of the EU's Digital Omnibus simplification agenda, to streamline parts of the EU AI Act ahead of key compliance deadlines.
 
The provisional agreement revises key high-risk deadlines: 2 December 2027 for standalone high-risk AI systems listed in Annex III (for example, those used in employment, education, biometrics, critical infrastructure, and migration and border contexts).
 
Many providers and deployers were not on course to meet the 2 August 2026 deadline, and the harmonised standards needed to support conformity assessments were not yet in place. Regulators are likely to treat the extended deadlines as an opportunity for businesses to build structured compliance programmes, rather than as permission to defer action.


- **Source:** Council of the EU / European Parliament — Official press release, 7 May 2026 | Mishcon de Reya legal analysis, May 2026
- **Evidence type:** Primary (official legislative announcement)

### D2 — EU Commission Publishes Code of Practice on AI Content Marking

On 10 June 2026, the European Commission published a Code of Practice on marking and labelling AI-generated content.
 
Anyone shipping a generative AI feature into the EU market needs UI labelling, machine-readable metadata embedding, and detection capability operational by 2 December 2026.

### D3 — UK Financial Regulators Tighten AI Accountability Expectations

The FCA, PRA and Bank of England have consistently signalled that AI will be overseen through existing regulatory frameworks, rather than through bespoke AI-specific rules, while political scrutiny is intensifying, supervisory expectations are rising, and the regulators are investing heavily in sandbox initiatives and long-term reviews.
 
In January 2026, the House of Commons Treasury Committee published its report on AI in financial services. Despite 75% of UK financial services firms now using AI, the Committee concluded that regulators' approach left consumers and the financial system exposed to potentially serious harm.
 
That same month, the FCA launched the Mills Review, examining the long-term impact of AI on retail financial services. The Review introduced the concept of a "proxy economy," in which consumers increasingly interact with financial services through AI intermediaries rather than directly with firms. Recommendations are expected to reach the FCA Board in summer 2026.


- **Source:** House of Commons Treasury Committee, January 2026; FCA Mills Review, January 2026; BoE/PRA response, April 2026
- **Evidence type:** Primary (parliamentary report, regulatory announcement)
- **Confidence:** ✅ High

### D4 — South Africa Draft National AI Policy Enters Public Consultation

The publication of South Africa's Draft National Artificial Intelligence Policy in April 2026 marks an important shift from conceptual discussions on AI toward a structured national governance framework. Rather than proposing immediate, technology-specific regulation, the draft policy lays down the principles, institutions and implementation pathways that will guide AI development across sectors.
 
Government has opted for a sector-specific, multi-regulator model rather than a single AI regulator, meaning AI governance will be embedded within existing supervisory frameworks.
 
The publication of the draft policy on 10 April 2026 initiated a formal public consultation phase, with written comments invited for a period of 60 days, with submissions due by 10 June 2026.


- **Source:** South Africa Department of Communications and Digital Technologies — Government Gazette Notice 3880, 10 April 2026; Baker McKenzie, February 2026; Adams & Adams, April 2026

### D5 — OECD/AU Nairobi Workshop Identifies Africa's AI Governance Priorities

An OECD case study draws on findings from a workshop held in Nairobi, Kenya (10–11 March 2026), organised with the technical and financial contributions of the Agence Française de Développement and the UK's Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office, which convened government representatives and experts from 12 African countries.
 
Africa is moving from foundational privacy laws to active, enforceable AI regulation, with 16 countries now running national AI strategies and major countries including Nigeria, Angola, Morocco, and Namibia advancing binding laws.
 
45 African nations have now enacted data protection legislation, with 39 regulatory authorities fully operational. Regulators are increasingly mandating Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs) and Algorithmic Impact Assessments (AIAs), elevating the cost of non-compliance.


- **Source:** OECD AI Governance in Africa Case Study, April 2026; Yellow Card 2026 Report on Data Protection and AI Governance in Africa, April 2026

### D6 — Enterprise AI Governance Maturity Gaps Persist Across Sectors

McKinsey's March 2026 survey found nearly two-thirds of organisations cite security and risk concerns as the main barrier to expanding their use of AI agents, and that across almost every risk category, firms are better at identifying the risk than they are at actually managing it.
 
More than half of organisations lack a systematic register of what AI systems are in production or development.
 
The EU AI Act elevates AI governance to board-level responsibility. Directors face potential personal liability under corporate law fiduciary duties if they consciously disregard significant regulatory risks. Effective board oversight requires clear accountability assignment, regular AI risk reporting, and AI literacy ensuring directors receive sufficient training.


- **Source:** McKinsey 2026 AI Trust Maturity Survey, March 2026; SecurePrivacy enterprise compliance analysis, April 2026; Fusefy enterprise compliance guide, May 2026
- **Evidence type:** Secondary analyst (McKinsey); specialist trade press
- **Confidence:** Medium (survey-based; methodology not fully disclosed)

## 5. Market Implications

**BFSI:** 
Under SMCR, a named senior manager is personally liable for what AI systems do within their area of responsibility. The FCA has confirmed that handing a decision to an algorithm does not transfer that liability.
 UK and Irish financial services firms face an immediate governance documentation and audit-trail burden. 
Firms raised concerns about whether traditional model risk management and validation approaches can scale effectively in the context of widespread deployment of generative and agentic AI systems, and questioned how the concept of a "human-in-the-loop" can be meaningfully applied as AI systems take on more decision-making functions.


**Pharma:** 
In March 2025, EMA, together with FDA, formally defined ten guiding principles for good AI practice in drug development. These principles stress that AI should be used responsibly in evidence generation, with patient safety paramount, covering all phases from discovery to pharmacovigilance.
 Pharma deployments classified as high-risk under the EU AI Act now benefit from the Omnibus deadline extension to December 2027, but EMA alignment requirements remain in force.

**Energy & Retail/FMCG:** AI systems used in critical infrastructure management (energy) and employment/HR analytics (retail, FMCG) fall under Annex III high-risk classifications. 
Under the Digital Omnibus, obligations for AI systems used in areas including biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, migration, asylum and border control will now apply from 2nd December 2027.
 The delay provides a finite planning window — not a deferral.

**CMT/Technology:** 
By Q1 2026, EU member states issued 50 fines totalling €250 million, primarily for GPAI non-compliance. Ireland handles 60% of cases due to the location of tech company headquarters.
 CMT and technology firms headquartered in Ireland face disproportionate enforcement exposure.

**Africa (Cross-sector):** 
Startups face growing compliance risks, such as fines, mandatory data localisation, and stricter oversight in sectors like finance and healthcare.
 Multinationals entering or scaling in African markets should assess each jurisdiction's AI governance maturity, noting the divergent pace of Nigeria, South Africa, Kenya, and Morocco.
