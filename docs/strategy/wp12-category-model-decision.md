**Method:** Validate-and-tighten stance; argue both sides; optimise for clarity, ownership and long-term defensibility over preserving prior assumptions.

## Direct answer (lead with the recommendation)

**Adopt Model C.**

- **Mission / front door:** AI Readiness
- **Category to own:** **Decision Performance**
- **Proprietary engine (moat):** **Constraint Intelligence**
- **Discipline (internal heritage):** Decision Intelligence — kept as method, *not* marketed as the category
- **Products under the category:** DPI · Signals · Assessments · Benchmarks · Research

**Critical question, answered directly: Constraint Intelligence is the proprietary engine that powers the Decision Performance category. It is *not* the category.**

This means I am **partially retracting my own Part 3 statement** ("Constraint Intelligence is the platform's product"). On pressure-testing, that over-rotated. Constraint Intelligence is the *mechanism*; Decision Performance is the *product category*. Your instinct to challenge it was correct, and Model C captures the better articulation.

---

## First, the real problem: four concepts or four altitudes?

The strategic risk you named — *too many competing concepts* — is real, but the four concepts only *compete* if they sit at the same altitude. They do not have to. Model C resolves the complexity not by deleting concepts but by assigning each a distinct, non-competing role:

```
   ALTITUDE            CONCEPT                 ROLE                       MARKET-FACING?
   ─────────           ───────                 ────                       ──────────────
   Mission       →     AI Readiness            the promise / front door   YES (the hook)
   Category      →     Decision Performance    what we own & sell         YES (the category)
   Engine        →     Constraint Intelligence the proprietary mechanism  NO  (the moat, mostly hidden)
   Discipline    →     Decision Intelligence   internal method / heritage NO  (rarely surfaced)
```

Read this way, the **market-facing surface is just two ideas** — *become AI Ready by improving your Decision Performance* — which is a **reduction** in complexity, not an increase. Constraint Intelligence and Decision Intelligence move below the waterline. This is the single most important reframing in this decision: **the goal is not fewer concepts, it is fewer concepts *the market has to learn*.**

## The critical question — both sides argued

### "Constraint Intelligence IS the category" — the case FOR
- It is the genuinely original idea in the whole programme; constraint-led diagnosis is the unique point of view.
- White space: no incumbent owns "Constraint Intelligence" in the enterprise-AI sense — first-mover category creation.
- The constraint dataset has powerful data network effects, so whoever owns the *category* owns a compounding moat.
- Memorable and ownable as a term.

### "Constraint Intelligence IS the category" — the case AGAINST
- Executives don't shop for constraints; near-zero existing search demand means you fund expensive category education from scratch.
- It reads as a method/feature, not a destination — products sit *awkwardly* under it ("the DPI, a Constraint Intelligence product"? clumsy).
- It is a *negative* frame (barriers) where the buyer wants a *positive* one (performance, readiness).
- It reintroduces a fourth headline concept — the precise complexity WP12 is chartered to cut.

### "Constraint Intelligence is the ENGINE" — the case FOR
- Lets the moat be a moat without paying category-creation tax. You market the legible outcome (Decision Performance) and defend with the hidden mechanism (Constraint Intelligence).
- Keeps the market story to two learnable ideas.
- Engines are *more* defensible unnamed-or-lightly-named: competitors can copy a positioning faster than they can copy a mechanism + the dataset feeding it.
- Precedent: category = "search", engine = PageRank; category = "CRM", engine = the data model. Nobody bought PageRank; everybody bought search.

### "Constraint Intelligence is the engine" — the risk, and the mitigation
- Risk: burying it means under-investing in the unique POV publicly and ceding the constraint-led narrative.
- Mitigation: you can still **name and publish** Constraint Intelligence as your *signature methodology/engine* (exactly as Google publishes "PageRank" for authority) — thought leadership and research without making it the category executives must learn to buy.

**Verdict:** the engine reading wins on every axis that matters for clarity, commercial fit and complexity-reduction, and *loses nothing* on defensibility (the moat is intact either way). Constraint Intelligence = **engine**.

---

## Recommendation, rejections, reasoning

**RECOMMEND — Model C.** Decision Performance is the category (legible, ownable, already your measured outcome, brand-aligned). Constraint Intelligence is the proprietary engine (the moat, named for authority but not asked to carry the market). AI Readiness is the mission/front door (demand capture). Decision Intelligence is retained as internal discipline/heritage. Score 44/50, and it is the only model that *reduces* market-facing complexity.

**REJECT — Model A.** Decision Intelligence is a contested, analyst-owned term; building the brand on it means renting a category Gartner and Google already define. 20/50.

**REJECT — Model B.** Constraint Intelligence as the headline category fails the executive-clarity and complexity tests, carries a negative frame, and would force expensive category creation — while delivering no more defensibility than Model C, which gets the same moat without those costs. 32/50.

## One honest caveat
The weakest point in this recommendation is **brand–category drift**: the name *TheAIReadyist* anchors on the mission layer (Readiness) while the owned category sits one layer down (Performance). Model C manages this (Readiness as front door → Performance as category is a clean funnel), but it is a tension to name openly in D6, not bury.
