EU AI Act — Enforcement & Risk

Who enforces the Act, how they will do it, and what your real exposure looks like in 2026 and beyond.

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689
Full enforcement from Aug 2026
Updated June 2026
Who Enforces the Act
European Commission
AI Office

Sits inside the Commission. Responsible for general-purpose AI (GPAI) models — the foundation models underlying most enterprise AI tooling. Full enforcement powers active from August 2026.

  • Supervises GPAI model providers (GPT, Claude, Gemini etc.)
  • Powers to request documentation, conduct evaluations, impose fines
  • Can issue market restriction and recall orders
  • Operates the AI Act Whistleblower Tool
Member State Level
National Market Surveillance Authorities

One per member state, minimum. Responsible for High Risk AI systems deployed within their jurisdiction. Enforcement quality and appetite will vary significantly by country.

  • Investigate complaints and incidents within their territory
  • Conduct market surveillance of High Risk systems
  • Impose national fines and sanctions
  • Only 8 of 27 member states had designated contact points by March 2026
Oversight Bodies
Scientific Panel & AI Board

The Scientific Panel of independent experts advises on GPAI risks. The European AI Board coordinates national authorities and promotes consistent enforcement across member states.

  • Scientific Panel can alert the AI Office to systemic risks
  • AI Board issues guidelines and recommendations
  • National DPAs retain GDPR enforcement powers — overlap is real
  • Sector regulators (financial, health, transport) may act in parallel

How Enforcement Actually Happens
Route 01
Incident Reporting

Providers and deployers must report serious incidents and malfunctions to national authorities. A discriminatory hiring algorithm, a failed credit model, a clinical AI involved in patient harm — each creates a mandatory reporting obligation that places the organisation directly in front of regulators.

Active Aug 2026
Route 02
Whistleblower Reports

From August 2026, EU AI Act violations fall within the Whistleblowing Directive. Employees and contractors have legally protected channels to report non-compliance directly to the AI Office. The tool is already live. Legal and HR need to own this risk — not just the CIO.

Active Aug 2026
Route 03
Procurement Blocking

Enterprise buyers are already requiring AI Act conformity declarations, AI governance addenda to contracts, and incident SLAs. An organisation that cannot produce documentation loses contracts before it faces fines. For vendors selling into financial services, healthcare or public sector — this is happening now.

Active now
Route 04
Competitor Complaints

Any natural or legal person may lodge a complaint with a national authority. In competitive markets, well-compliant organisations have a structural incentive to report peers cutting corners. This is the same dynamic that drove early GDPR enforcement actions in financial services and adtech.

Active Aug 2026
Enforcement Timeline
Feb 2025
Prohibited AI bans in force

Tier 1 systems must be decommissioned. No grace period. Criminal referral possible in some jurisdictions.

Aug 2025
GPAI obligations begin

General-purpose AI model obligations apply. AI Office enforcement powers follow one year later.

Jun 2026
Documentation trail forming

The absence of an inventory, classification, or governance owner is already a future liability. The window for a defensible position is open.

Aug 2026
Full High Risk enforcement

All High Risk obligations active. GPAI enforcement powers live. Whistleblower Directive extended. MSAs empowered to fine.

2027–28
First enforcement wave

Based on GDPR precedent: first significant fines, public findings, market withdrawal orders. Documentation from 2026 will be scrutinised.

△
Standards gap

The harmonised standards that define what High Risk compliance actually looks like have not yet been published. First delivery expected late 2026. This creates uncertainty for both organisations and regulators — but does not suspend the obligation.

!
GDPR overlap

Where AI systems process personal data, GDPR and the AI Act apply simultaneously. Different authorities may investigate the same organisation under each law at the same time. A single incident can trigger both regimes — and both penalty structures.

The Executive Position
Where you stand — honestly

The short-term probability of a large fine is low. The enforcement infrastructure is not yet fully operational. Most national authorities are still staffing up. The harmonised standards are delayed. Regulators cannot easily fine a business for failing to meet a standard that does not exist.

The Digital Omnibus may push the effective High Risk deadline to late 2027. That is not confirmed. The original August 2026 deadline remains legally binding.

The medium-term enforcement risk is material. Based on GDPR precedent, enforcement reaches operational velocity two to three years after obligations come into force. The documentation trail you create — or fail to create — between now and August 2026 will be the primary evidence base when investigators arrive.

The organisations that will be first in scope are those with documented High Risk systems, no conformity assessment, no named accountability owner, and no audit trail. That description currently fits most large enterprises.
