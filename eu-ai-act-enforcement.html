How the EU AI Act Will Actually Be Policed

The penalty numbers are real. But a CIO or CFO asking how likely they are to face one deserves a more honest answer than the regulation's headline figures suggest. Here is what enforcement actually looks like in 2026.

The enforcement architecture is fragmented — by design

The Act establishes a two-tiered governance structure: national competent authorities (NCAs) handle market surveillance for AI systems deployed within their jurisdiction, while the European AI Office, sitting inside the Commission, oversees general-purpose AI models and cross-border cases. In theory, clean. In practice, as of March 2026, only eight of the 27 member states had designated a functioning single point of contact. Twelve missed the competent authority appointment deadline entirely.

This matters because enforcement intensity will vary significantly by country — exactly as it did with GDPR. Organisations operating across multiple EU jurisdictions will face different investigative standards, different documentation expectations, and different regulatory appetite depending on where their national supervisor sits.

The standards that define compliance don't exist yet

Here is the structural problem that no one in the compliance industry is advertising loudly enough. The harmonised standards that provide legal certainty for High Risk compliance — commissioned from European standardisation bodies in 2023 — were not delivered on schedule. The standardisation work is still ongoing, and first publications may not arrive until late 2026. Without those standards, both organisations and regulators are navigating by instinct. An authority cannot easily fine a business for failing to meet a standard that has not been published.

The Digital Omnibus proposal, tabled by the Commission in November 2025, explicitly proposes linking the application date for High Risk obligations to the availability of those standards — putting the effective deadline at risk of slipping to late 2027. The original August 2026 deadline remains legally binding unless Parliament and Council agree otherwise.

The executive who uses "it might be delayed" as a reason not to act is taking a board-level gamble. The delay is not confirmed. The liability window is already open. Non-compliance findings are public, and the documentation trail — or absence of one — you are creating now will matter when enforcement reaches operational velocity.

The real enforcement triggers — and they're not what you expect

Waiting for a regulator to knock on the door is the wrong mental model. The more likely first-wave enforcement routes are more structural — and several are already active.

Trigger 01
Incident-driven reporting

Providers and deployers are required to report serious incidents and malfunctions to authorities. A recruitment algorithm that demonstrably discriminates, a credit model that fails in an auditable way, a clinical decision tool involved in a patient harm event — these create mandatory reporting obligations that put organisations directly in front of regulators without anyone filing a complaint.

Trigger 02
Whistleblower-triggered

From August 2026, AI Act violations fall within the scope of the EU Whistleblowing Directive, giving employees and contractors legally protected channels to report suspected non-compliance. The AI Office has already launched a dedicated whistleblower tool. For any organisation with staff aware of AI governance gaps, this is a material risk that legal and HR need to own now — not the CIO alone.

Trigger 03
Procurement-blocked

Enterprise procurement is already asking vendors for AI Act conformity declarations, SOC 2 AI addenda, and incident SLAs. An organisation that cannot produce documentation of its AI governance posture risks losing contracts, not just receiving fines. For B2B software vendors selling into regulated industries, customers are actively exporting their regulatory burden through contract requirements.

Trigger 04
Competitor-complained

The Act grants any natural or legal person the right to lodge a complaint with a national authority. In competitive markets — financial services, insurance, healthcare — this creates a structural incentive for well-compliant competitors to report peers who are cutting corners. This is not theoretical; it is the same dynamic that drove early GDPR enforcement actions.

The GDPR comparison — what it tells you, and what it doesn't

The natural comparator is GDPR. Its lesson: enforcement was slow to start, then accelerated sharply once national authorities built capability and political will. The first major GDPR fines arrived two to three years after the regulation came into force. The AI Act's maximum penalty of €35 million or 7% of global turnover for prohibited practices significantly exceeds GDPR's maximum of €20 million or 4% — a deliberate signal that the EU intends to treat AI regulation as the higher priority.

But there is a critical structural difference. GDPR enforcement largely started with complaints from data subjects. AI Act enforcement has more embedded triggers — incident reporting obligations, registration databases, conformity assessments that generate a paper trail. Regulators will not need to wait for a complaint. They can identify non-compliant organisations from database gaps alone.

The enforcement timeline — honestly stated

Feb 2025
Prohibited AI bans active

Tier 1 (Unacceptable Risk) prohibitions in force. No grace period. Decommissioning required immediately for any system in scope.

Aug 2025
GPAI obligations begin

General-purpose AI model obligations apply. Enforcement powers against GPAI providers not yet active — one year adjustment period in effect.

Now
Documentation trail forming

The absence of a system inventory, classification rationale, or governance owner is already a future liability. 78% of organisations have not acted. The window for a defensible position is open.

Aug 2026
High Risk obligations and full enforcement powers

All High Risk obligations apply. GPAI enforcement powers active. Whistleblower Directive protection extended to AI Act breaches. National MSAs empowered to fine. Procurement blocking intensifies.

2027–28
First enforcement wave likely

Based on the GDPR precedent: first significant fines, public non-compliance findings, and market withdrawal orders. Organisations without documentation from 2026 will be exposed.

The executive summary

The probability of a large fine landing on your organisation in the next 12 months is low. The enforcement infrastructure is not yet fully operational, the harmonised standards are delayed, and national authorities are still staffing up.

The probability of enforcement reaching operational velocity by 2028 — with organisations selected for investigation partly based on the documentation trail, or absence of one, they are creating right now — is high.

78% of organisations have not taken meaningful compliance steps. That is both a warning and an opportunity. Organisations that move now will have the classification rationale, governance structures, and audit trail in place before regulators have the capacity to investigate. The ones that wait will be responding to enforcement rather than anticipating it.

The window to build a defensible position on your own terms is open. It will not stay open indefinitely.
